### Step 1: Defining the Problem Shift (The Core Change)

The question pivots on the concept of **Nonzero-Sum** games.

**Hint 1:**

What is the fundamental difference between a standard Minimax game (like Chess or Tic-Tac-Toe) and a Nonzero-Sum game?

- In Chess, if you gain +1 point (you win), your opponent loses -1 point. The sum of utility is always zero: $+1 + (-1) = 0$. This is called a **Zero-Sum** game.
    
- In the game described, two players, Player 1 and Player 2, have **distinct utility functions**.
    

**Question for you:** Given that the players have _distinct_ utility functions, what does a state (a terminal node) in the search tree need to store, and how does this affect the core Minimax rule (Maximize for MAX, Minimize for MIN)?





### Step 2 The Core Change: Utility Vectors

In the standard **Zero-Sum** Minimax:

- A terminal node (the end of the game) has **one** value: `V`.
    
- If Player 1 gets `V`, Player 2 automatically gets `-V`.
    

In the **Nonzero-Sum** game described in the question:

- The players have **distinct utility functions**.
    
- This means Player 1's gain is **not necessarily** Player 2's loss.
    

**The necessary change:**

Instead of a single value `V`, every node (and especially the terminal nodes) must now store a **vector (or pair) of utility values**—one for each player.

$$\text{Terminal Node Value} = \langle U_1, U_2 \rangle$$

- $U_1$: Utility for Player 1 (the maximizing player for this utility).
    
- $U_2$: Utility for Player 2 (the maximizing player for this utility).
    

This is the biggest structural change!

### Step 3: The Minimax Rule

Now that we have two values, we can't just `MAX` or `MIN` a single number.

**Question for you:**

If it is **Player 1's turn** (the MAX player in standard Minimax), what value from the vector $\langle U_1, U_2 \rangle$ should they be trying to maximize? And what value should they ignore for their _decision-making_ at that step?

Exactly! If it's Player 1's turn, they are only concerned with maximizing $U_1$.

However, because the algorithm needs to return the _full_ utility vector to the parent node, the structure of Minimax becomes a bit different.

### Step 4: Vector Minimax (Nonzero-Sum Minimax)

In Zero-Sum Minimax, the operation is simple:

$$\text{MINIMAX}(\text{node}) = \begin{cases} \text{MAX} (\text{child values}) & \text{if MAX's turn} \\ \text{MIN} (\text{child values}) & \text{if MIN's turn} \end{cases}$$

In Nonzero-Sum Vector Minimax, the **choice** is based on one utility, but the **result** carries both utilities.

Let's assume:

- $P_1$ is the current player.
    
- The options are the resulting vectors from the child nodes: $\langle U_{1a}, U_{2a} \rangle, \langle U_{1b}, U_{2b} \rangle, \dots$
    

The new Decision Rule:

$P_1$ will choose the child that has the highest $U_1$ value.

$$\text{VECTOR-MINIMAX}(\text{node}) = \text{The vector }\langle U_{1i}, U_{2i} \rangle \text{ associated with } \max_{i} (U_{1i})$$

**Question for you (This is the critical part for the exam):**

If Player 1 (the current player) chooses the path that maximizes $U_1$, what happens to the value $U_2$ (the opponent's utility) in the final resulting vector? Does Player 1 care about minimizing $U_2$ like they do in Zero-Sum Minimax?
That is absolutely correct! **They do not care about minimizing $U_2$.**

In a Nonzero-Sum game, Player 1's goal is purely **selfish**: to maximize their own payoff, $U_1$. The opponent's payoff, $U_2$, is simply a consequence of the chosen move. Player 1 treats $U_2$ as a necessary piece of information to pass back up the tree, but $U_2$ does not factor into _which move_ $P_1$ chooses.

### Summary of Minimax Change

| **Feature**           | **Zero-Sum Minimax**                                                    | **Nonzero-Sum Vector Minimax**             |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| **Node Value**        | Single scalar $V$                                                       | Vector $\langle U_1, U_2 \rangle$          |
| **Player $i$'s Rule** | Choose move that maximizes $U_i$ (which automatically minimizes $U_j$). | Choose move that maximizes **only** $U_i$. |
| **Opponent's Value**  | $U_j = -U_i$                                                            | $U_j$ is independent of $U_i$.             |

This handles the first part of the question: "Describe how the minimax... algorithm change for two-player, nonzero-sum games..."

## Part 2: Alpha-Beta Pruning

Now, we move to the next layer of complexity: **Alpha-Beta Pruning**.

In standard Zero-Sum Alpha-Beta, the pruning works because of the direct conflict: if a branch is bad for the maximizer ($\alpha$), it means it's good for the minimizer ($\beta$), and if the opponent (minimizer) has _already_ found a better guaranteed result elsewhere, the current branch can be cut. The **single value** $V$ lets us compare the best result found so far ($\alpha$ or $\beta$).

**Question for you:**

Given that we are now dealing with **two independent utility values** $\langle U_1, U_2 \rangle$, why is the standard Alpha-Beta pruning rule—which relies on one player's worst-case payoff being better than the current best-case cutoff—no longer guaranteed to work?


Here is the **simple reason** why standard Alpha-Beta pruning fails in Nonzero-Sum games:

### The Pruning Failure (Simple Explanation)

Pruning works because of the **perfect conflict** in Zero-Sum games.

|**Zero-Sum Game (Chess)**|**Nonzero-Sum Game (The Problem)**|
|---|---|
|**Only 1 Score:** The board has a single score, $V$.|**Two Scores:** The board has two independent scores, $\langle U_1, U_2 \rangle$.|
|**The Rule:** $U_2 = -U_1$. (My gain is your loss.)|**The Rule:** $U_2$ has no relationship to $U_1$. (My gain might be your gain too, or irrelevant to your score.)|
|**Pruning Logic:** If MAX finds a path guaranteeing $V \geq 10$, and MIN finds an alternate move forcing the score to be $V \leq 5$, MAX **instantly prunes** the $V \leq 5$ path. Why? Because MAX knows the opponent (MIN) will choose the move that gives $V \leq 5$, and $10$ is better than $5$.|**Pruning Logic Fails:** MAX is only maximizing $U_1$. When exploring a new branch, MAX sees $U_1=5$ and $U_2=1000$. MAX must discard this branch because $5$ is worse than the current $\alpha_1$. However, this new branch (with $U_2=1000$) **might later lead** to a path that gives $U_1=50$ and $U_2=1001$.|
|**Crux:** When MAX makes a move, they know _exactly_ what MIN will do because MIN will choose to minimize $V$. This predictability creates the single cutoff.|**Crux:** When MAX makes a move, they know MIN will choose the move that maximizes $U_2$. Since $U_2$ is independent of $U_1$, **MAX cannot use $U_2$'s current maximum value ($\beta$) to set a boundary for what $U_1$'s value might be deeper in that branch.**|

**In short:** Standard Alpha-Beta relies on the utility landscape being a single, inverse slope. When you have two independent utility functions, the landscape turns into a complicated 3D surface, and the simple Alpha-Beta cutoff no longer works.

---



### TLDR: The Pruning Failure

**Yes.** In Nonzero-Sum games, the fundamental reason Alpha-Beta Pruning fails is:

> **The opponent (MIN in the old terminology, $P_2$ now) is only trying to maximize their own score ($U_2$). They are _not_ trying to minimize our score ($U_1$).**

Because their goal is independent of ours, we cannot use their guaranteed best move ($\beta$) to set a meaningful ceiling ($\alpha$) on our own potential payoff. The single-value cutoff ($\alpha$/$\beta$) is useless when we have a two-dimensional utility vector.

### Part 3: The Cooperative Constraint (The Final Solution)

Now that we know why pruning _fails_ without constraints, let's look at the final part of the question—the key to re-enabling pruning:

> _What if the player's utility functions on any state differ by at most a constant k, making the game **almost cooperative**?_

This constraint, $|U_1 - U_2| \leq k$, means $U_1$ and $U_2$ are always coupled.

**Final Question for you (connecting the math to the game theory):**

In this _almost cooperative_ game, what is the player's goal now?

1. Does $P_1$ still try to maximize **only** $U_1$? (Purely selfish.)
    
2. Or does $P_1$ now try to maximize **both** $U_1$ and $U_2$ (since the game is "almost cooperative")?

That is the perfect final piece of the puzzle!

### Step 6: The "Almost Cooperative" Goal

The player's goal does **not** change, even in the "almost cooperative" game:

- **Player 1 still only tries to maximize $U_1$.**
    
- **Player 2 still only tries to maximize $U_2$.**
    

The game is only "almost cooperative" in the sense that their utility functions are _mathematically related_, which makes large conflicts impossible. They are still fundamentally self-interested agents.

However, this mathematical constraint $|U_1 - U_2| \leq k$ is all we need to **re-enable pruning**.

### The Final Pruning Rule (Step 7: The Answer)

Because of the constraint, we can establish the necessary cutoffs (bounds) for $U_1$ using information about $U_2$, and vice versa.

Let's use the inequality we derived earlier:

$$U_1 \leq U_2 + k$$

**Pruning Condition (MAX's Turn):**

1. Let $\alpha_1$ be the best $U_1$ score Player 1 has **already guaranteed** in a previous branch.
    
2. Player 1 explores a new branch and finds a state where the opponent ($P_2$) is guaranteed to achieve **at most** $\beta_2$.
    
3. $P_1$ can calculate their own absolute maximum potential score on this new branch: $U_{1, \text{max}} = \beta_2 + k$.
    

**If $U_{1, \text{max}} < \alpha_1$, the branch can be pruned.**

- **Why?** Because even if everything goes absolutely perfectly for $P_1$ on this new branch (which would mean $P_2$'s score reaches $\beta_2$), the best $P_1$ can achieve is $U_{1, \text{max}}$, and $P_1$ has already found $\alpha_1$, which is better. Therefore, the branch is useless.
    

**This is the core of the answer to the final part of the question.** The constraint $k$ transforms the independent utility vector back into a space where an effective cutoff (and thus pruning) is possible.
