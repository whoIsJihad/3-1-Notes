# Lecture Notes: Joint, Conditional, and Marginal Distributions

This document covers the core concepts related to probability distributions defined over multiple random variables (RVs).

## 1. Joint Distribution

When dealing with a system described by two or more random variables, their interaction and simultaneous probabilities are captured by a **Joint Distribution**.

### 1.1 Joint Cumulative Distribution Function (Joint CDF)

The most fundamental way to define a joint distribution for two random variables $X$ and $Y$ is through their Joint CDF, $F_{X,Y}(x,y)$.

**Definition:**

$$F_{X,Y}(x,y) = P(X \le x, Y \le y)$$

This gives the probability that $X$ takes a value less than or equal to $x$ **AND** $Y$ takes a value less than or equal to $y$.

### 1.2 Joint Probability Mass Function (Joint PMF) - (Discrete RVs)

If $X$ and $Y$ are discrete random variables, their joint behavior is described by the Joint PMF.

**Definition:**

$$P(X=x, Y=y)$$

This is the probability that $X$ simultaneously takes the specific value $x$ and $Y$ simultaneously takes the specific value $y$.

**Properties:**

1. $0 \le P(X=x, Y=y) \le 1$ (No negative probability).
    
2. The sum over all possible pairs $(x, y)$ must equal 1:
    
    $$\sum_{x} \sum_{y} P(X=x, Y=y) = 1$$

_**Example (from your notes):**_ The table below represents a valid Joint PMF, confirming both properties (non-negative and sums to 1):

||$\mathbf{Y=0}$|$\mathbf{Y=1}$|
|---|---|---|
|$\mathbf{X=0}$|$\frac{1}{3}$|$\frac{1}{6}$|
|$\mathbf{X=1}$|$\frac{1}{3}$|$\frac{1}{6}$|

$$\text{Total Sum} = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{2}{3} + \frac{2}{6} = \frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = 1.$$

### 1.3 Joint Probability Density Function (Joint PDF) - (Continuous RVs)

If $X$ and $Y$ are continuous random variables, their joint behavior is described by the Joint PDF, $f_{X,Y}(x,y)$.

**Definition:**

$$P((X,Y) \in B) = \iint_{B} f_{X,Y}(x,y) dx dy$$

where $B$ is any region (like a circle, rectangle, etc.) in the 2D plane.

**Properties:**

1. $f_{X,Y}(x,y) \ge 0$.
    
2. The total integral over the entire plane must equal 1:
    
    $$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{X,Y}(x,y) dx dy = 1$$

**Intuition:** The Joint PDF tells you the _relative likelihood_ of the pair $(X, Y)$ falling at a specific coordinate $(x, y)$.

_**Example (Uniform on a Square**_ $\mathcal{S}=[0,1] \times [0,1]$_**):**_ Since the distribution is uniform on the unit square (area $1 \times 1 = 1$), the constant PDF value $c$ must satisfy $\iint_{\mathcal{S}} c \, dx dy = 1$.

$$\text{Area} \cdot c = 1 \implies 1 \cdot c = 1 \implies c = 1.$$$$f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\begin{cases} 1 & 0 \le x \le 1 \text{ and } 0 \le y \le 1 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

## 2. Marginal Distributions (Getting Marginals)

The Marginal Distribution of one variable (e.g., $X$) is simply its individual distribution, ignoring the value of the other variable ($Y$). It's the "margin" total of the joint distribution.

### 2.1 Marginal PMF (Discrete Case)

To find the Marginal PMF $P(X=x)$, we sum the Joint PMF over all possible values of $Y$:

$$P(X=x) = \sum_{y} P(X=x, Y=y)$$

_**Example (using the table above):**_

- **Marginal** $P(X=0)$**:** Sum across the $X=0$ row: $\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{3}{6} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{2}}$.
    
- **Marginal** $P(Y=0)$**:** Sum down the $Y=0$ column: $\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = \mathbf{\frac{2}{3}}$.
    

### 2.2 Marginal PDF (Continuous Case)

To find the Marginal PDF $f_X(x)$, we integrate the Joint PDF over the entire range of the other variable $Y$:

$$f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} f_{X,Y}(x,y) dy$$

Similarly, for $Y$: $f_Y(y) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} f_{X,Y}(x,y) dx$.

_**Example (Uniform on a Square):**_ For $0 \le x \le 1$:

$$f_X(x) = \int_{0}^{1} f_{X,Y}(x,y) dy = \int_{0}^{1} 1 \, dy = [y]_0^1 = 1.$$

Thus, $X \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$, which makes intuitive sense!

## 3. Independence of Random Variables

Two random variables, $X$ and $Y$, are said to be **independent** if knowledge of one does not change the probability distribution of the other.

This is formally defined by checking if the joint distribution is simply the product of the marginal distributions.

### Independence Condition (General)

$$F_{X,Y}(x,y) = F_X(x) F_Y(y) \quad \text{for all } x, y$$

### Independence Condition (PMF/PDF)

- **Discrete (PMF):** $P(X=x, Y=y) = P(X=x) P(Y=y)$
    
- **Continuous (PDF):** $f_{X,Y}(x,y) = f_X(x) f_Y(y)$
    

_**Example Check (Uniform on a Square):**_ We found $f_{X,Y}(x,y) = 1$ and $f_X(x) = 1$, $f_Y(y) = 1$ (for $0 \le x, y \le 1$).

$$f_X(x) \cdot f_Y(y) = 1 \cdot 1 = 1 = f_{X,Y}(x,y).$$

**Conclusion:** $X$ and $Y$ are independent in the case of a uniform distribution on a rectangular/square region.

_**Example Check (Uniform on a Circle**_ $x^2 + y^2 \le 1$_**):**_

- Here, $f_{X,Y}(x,y) = \frac{1}{\pi}$ (since area of unit circle is $\pi$).
    
- The marginal distribution (from your notes' calculation, $f_X(x) = \frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{1-x^2}$) is **not** constant.
    
- $f_{X,Y}(x,y) = \frac{1}{\pi}$
    
- $f_X(x) f_Y(y) = \left(\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{1-x^2}\right) \left(\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{1-y^2}\right) = \frac{4}{\pi^2}\sqrt{(1-x^2)(1-y^2)}$
    

Since $f_{X,Y}(x,y) \ne f_X(x) f_Y(y)$, $X$ and $Y$ are **dependent**. **Intuition:** If you know $X=0.9$ (close to the edge), $Y$ is constrained to a very small range, meaning the value of $X$ tells you a lot about $Y$. This implies dependence.

## 4. Conditional Distributions

When $X$ and $Y$ are dependent, we want to know the distribution of one variable _given_ that the other variable has taken a fixed, observed value.

### 4.1 Conditional PMF (Discrete Case)

The probability of $Y$ taking value $y$ given $X$ has taken value $x$ is defined using the standard conditional probability formula:

$$P(Y=y \,|\, X=x) = \frac{P(X=x, Y=y)}{P(X=x)}$$$$\text{Conditional PMF} = \frac{\text{Joint PMF}}{\text{Marginal PMF of } X}$$

### 4.2 Conditional PDF (Continuous Case)

Similarly, the Conditional PDF of $Y$ given $X=x$ is:

$$f_{Y|X}(y|x) = \frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_X(x)}$$

**Intuition:** Once you fix $X=x$, you are essentially taking a "slice" of the joint distribution $f_{X,Y}(x,y)$. You must then divide by the height of the marginal $f_X(x)$ to ensure the resulting 1D distribution (in terms of $y$) integrates to 1.

_**Example (Uniform on a Circle):**_ We can find the Conditional PDF $f_{Y|X}(y|x)$ for the unit circle (where $x^2+y^2 \le 1$).

1. **Joint PDF:** $f_{X,Y}(x,y) = \frac{1}{\pi}$ (for $x^2+y^2 \le 1$).
    
2. **Marginal PDF:** $f_X(x) = \frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{1-x^2}$ (for $-1 \le x \le 1$).
    

For a fixed $x$, the range of $y$ is $[-\sqrt{1-x^2}, \sqrt{1-x^2}]$.

$$f_{Y|X}(y|x) = \frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_X(x)} = \frac{1/\pi}{2/\pi \sqrt{1-x^2}} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{1-x^2}}$$

**Conclusion:** The conditional distribution $Y|X=x$ is a **Uniform distribution** over the range $\left[-\sqrt{1-x^2}, \sqrt{1-x^2}\right]$.

$$Y|x \sim \text{Unif}\left(-\sqrt{1-x^2}, \sqrt{1-x^2}\right)$$

This confirms the intuition: if you fix $x$ on a circle, the possible values for $y$ form a uniform line segment (a cord) across the circle.
