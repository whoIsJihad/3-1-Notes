## 1. Expected Value of the Absolute Difference for Uniform Variables

**Problem:** Find $E[|X-Y|]$ where $X$ and $Y$ are Independent and Identically Distributed (i.i.d.) $X, Y \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$.

The Joint PDF $f_{X,Y}(x,y)$ is $1$ for $0 \le x, y \le 1$, and $0$ otherwise.

### Method A: Direct Integration (LOTUS)

The Expected Value of a function $g(X, Y)$ is defined by the Law of the Unconscious Statistician (LOTUS):

$$E[g(X, Y)] = \iint_{\mathbb{R}^2} g(x,y) f_{X,Y}(x,y) dx dy$$

Here, $g(X, Y) = |X-Y|$. Since $f_{X,Y}(x,y) = 1$ on the unit square $\mathcal{S}=[0, 1] \times [0, 1]$, we integrate over $\mathcal{S}$.

$$E[|X-Y|] = \iint_{\mathcal{S}} |x-y| \cdot 1 \, dx dy$$

The function $|x-y|$ requires splitting the integration region into two parts:

1. Region $R_1$: where $x \ge y$, so $|x-y| = x-y$. (The lower triangle)
    
2. Region $R_2$: where $x < y$, so $|x-y| = y-x$. (The upper triangle)
    

$$E[|X-Y|] = \iint_{R_1} (x-y) dx dy + \iint_{R_2} (y-x) dx dy$$

Due to symmetry, $R_1$ and $R_2$ are symmetric regions, and the integrands $(x-y)$ and $(y-x)$ are symmetric counterparts. Therefore, $\iint_{R_2} (y-x) dx dy = \iint_{R_1} (x-y) dx dy$.

$$\text{So, } E[|X-Y|] = 2 \iint_{R_1} (x-y) dx dy$$

We integrate over $R_1$: $0 \le x \le 1$ and $0 \le y \le x$.

$$E[|X-Y|] = 2 \int_{x=0}^{1} \left[ \int_{y=0}^{x} (x-y) dy \right] dx$$

Inner Integral (w.r.t. $y$):

$$\int_{y=0}^{x} (x-y) dy = \left[ xy - \frac{y^2}{2} \right]_{y=0}^{x} = \left( x(x) - \frac{x^2}{2} \right) - (0) = x^2 - \frac{x^2}{2} = \frac{x^2}{2}$$

Outer Integral (w.r.t. $x$):

$$E[|X-Y|] = 2 \int_{0}^{1} \frac{x^2}{2} dx = \int_{0}^{1} x^2 dx$$

$$E[|X-Y|] = \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_{0}^{1} = \frac{1^3}{3} - 0 = \mathbf{\frac{1}{3}}$$

### Method B: Using Max and Min (Order Statistics)

Let $M = \max(X, Y)$ and $L = \min(X, Y)$.

Intuition: For any two numbers $x$ and $y$, the absolute difference $|x-y|$ is equal to the difference between the maximum and the minimum:

$$|X-Y| = M - L$$

Therefore, by linearity of expectation:

$$E[|X-Y|] = E[M - L] = E[M] - E[L]$$

We also know that the sum of the max and min must equal the sum of the original variables:

$$M + L = X + Y$$

Taking expectations:

$$E[M] + E[L] = E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]$$

Since $X \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$ and $Y \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$, we have $E[X] = E[Y] = 1/2$.

$$E[M] + E[L] = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} = \mathbf{1}$$

Now we have a system of two equations:

1. $E[M] + E[L] = 1$
    
2. $E[M] - E[L] = E[|X-Y|]$
    

To find $E[M]$ and $E[L]$ individually (a standard result for i.i.d. Uniform RVs):

- $E[M] = E[\max(X, Y)] = \frac{2}{3}$
    
- $E[L] = E[\min(X, Y)] = \frac{1}{3}$
    

(These values are derived from order statistics theory, where $E[X_{(k)}] = \frac{k}{n+1}$. For $n=2$: $E[L]=E[X_{(1)}] = 1/3$ and $E[M]=E[X_{(2)}] = 2/3$).

Substituting these values confirms the result:

$$E[|X-Y|] = E[M] - E[L] = \frac{2}{3} - \frac{1}{3} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{3}}$$

## 2. Expected Value of Absolute Value for Normal Variables

**Problem:** Find $E[|Z_1 - Z_2|]$ where $Z_1, Z_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ are i.i.d.

### Step 1: Distribution of the Difference $Y = Z_1 - Z_2$

A key property of independent Normal random variables is that their linear combination is also Normal.

Let $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_X, \sigma_X^2)$ and $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_Y, \sigma_Y^2)$ be independent.

$$aX + bY \sim \mathcal{N}(a\mu_X + b\mu_Y, a^2\sigma_X^2 + b^2\sigma_Y^2)$$

For $Y = Z_1 - Z_2$, we have $a=1, b=-1$, and $\mu_1 = 0, \mu_2 = 0, \sigma_1^2 = 1, \sigma_2^2 = 1$.

- **Mean:** $E[Y] = 1(0) + (-1)(0) = 0$.
    
- **Variance:** $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 1^2(1) + (-1)^2(1) = 1 + 1 = 2$.
    

$$\text{Thus, } Y = Z_1 - Z_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 2)$$

The standard deviation is $\sigma = \sqrt{2}$.

### Step 2: Calculate $E[|Y|]$

We need to calculate $E[|Y|]$ where $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 2)$. The PDF of $Y$ is:

$$f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} e^{-y^2/(2\sigma^2)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sqrt{2}} e^{-y^2/(2\cdot 2)} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}} e^{-y^2/4}$$

$$E[|Y|] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |y| f_Y(y) dy$$

Since the integrand $|y| f_Y(y)$ is an **even function**, we can integrate from $0$ to $\infty$ and multiply by 2:

$$E[|Y|] = 2 \int_{0}^{\infty} y \cdot \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}} e^{-y^2/4} dy = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} \int_{0}^{\infty} y e^{-y^2/4} dy$$

### Step 3: Use Substitution

Let $u = y^2/4$.

- $du = \frac{2y}{4} dy = \frac{y}{2} dy \implies y \, dy = 2 \, du$.
    
- Limits: When $y=0, u=0$. When $y=\infty, u=\infty$.
    

Substitute this into the integral:

$$E[|Y|] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-u} (2 \, du)$$

$$E[|Y|] = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-u} du$$

The integral $\int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-u} du$ is the definition of the Gamma function $\Gamma(1)$, which is equal to $\mathbf{1}$.

$$E[|Z_1 - Z_2|] = E[|Y|] = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \cdot 1 = \mathbf{\sqrt{\frac{4}{\pi}}} \quad \text{or} \quad \mathbf{\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}}$$

**_Alternative Method (Your Notes): Scaling a Standard Normal $Z$_**

You can express $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 2)$ as $Y = \sqrt{2} Z$, where $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.

$$E[|Y|] = E[|\sqrt{2} Z|] = \sqrt{2} E[|Z|]$$

We need to calculate $E[|Z|]$ where $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ (the standard normal).

$$E[|Z|] = 2 \int_{0}^{\infty} z \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-z^2/2} dz = \frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{0}^{\infty} z e^{-z^2/2} dz$$

Let $u = z^2/2$. Then $du = z \, dz$.

$$E[|Z|] = \frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-u} du = \frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \cdot 1 = \sqrt{\frac{4}{2\pi}} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}$$

Finally, substitute this back:

$$E[|Z_1 - Z_2|] = \sqrt{2} E[|Z|] = \sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} = \sqrt{\frac{4}{\pi}} = \mathbf{\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}}$$

Both methods lead to the same result, $\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}$ or $\sqrt{\frac{4}{\pi}}$.

## 3. Multinomial Distribution

The Multinomial distribution is a multivariate generalization of the Binomial distribution.

### 3.1 Story and Definition

**Binomial Story:** A fixed number of independent trials ($n$), with only two possible outcomes per trial (Success or Failure), where the probability of success ($p$) is constant.

- **RV:** X = count of successes. $X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$.
    

**Multinomial Story:** A fixed number of independent trials ($n$), where each trial can result in one of $k$ distinct outcomes. The probability of outcome $j$ is $p_j$, and $\sum_{j=1}^k p_j = 1$.

- **RV:** $\vec{X} = (X_1, X_2, \dots, X_k)$, where $X_j$ is the count of how many trials resulted in outcome $j$.
    
- **Constraints:** $\sum_{j=1}^k X_j = n$.
    

Example: Dice Throw

If you roll a fair 6-sided die $n=10$ times:

- $k=6$ possible outcomes (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6).
    
- $p_j = 1/6$ for all $j=1,\dots,6$.
    
- $\vec{X} = (X_1, X_2, X_3, X_4, X_5, X_6)$, where $X_3$ is the count of times a 3 was rolled.
    
- $X_1 + \dots + X_6 = 10$.
    

### 3.2 Joint PMF

The Multinomial PMF gives the probability of getting exactly $n_1$ occurrences of outcome 1, $n_2$ occurrences of outcome 2, ..., up to $n_k$ occurrences of outcome $k$, such that $\sum_{j=1}^k n_j = n$.

$$P(X_1=n_1, X_2=n_2, \dots, X_k=n_k) = \frac{n!}{n_1! n_2! \dots n_k!} p_1^{n_1} p_2^{n_2} \dots p_k^{n_k}$$

The two parts of the PMF:

1. **Probability of one specific sequence:** $p_1^{n_1} p_2^{n_2} \dots p_k^{n_k}$. (The probability of getting $n_1$ of outcome 1, then $n_2$ of outcome 2, etc.)
    
2. **Multinomial Coefficient:** $\frac{n!}{n_1! n_2! \dots n_k!}$. This counts the number of distinct ways (permutations) to arrange the $n$ trials to achieve the counts $n_1, n_2, \dots, n_k$.
    

### 3.3 Marginal Distribution

A fantastic property of the Multinomial distribution is that the marginal distribution of any component $X_j$ is always Binomial.

To find the distribution of $X_j$, we group the $k$ possible outcomes into two categories:

1. **Success:** Outcome $j$ occurs (with probability $p_j$).
    
2. **Failure:** Any outcome other than $j$ occurs (with probability $1 - p_j$).
    

Since we have $n$ independent trials, the count $X_j$ follows a Binomial distribution:

$$\mathbf{X_j \sim \text{Bin}(n, p_j)}$$

### 3.4 Moments of the Marginal Distribution

Since $X_j$ is Binomial, its moments are known:

- **Expected Value:** $E[X_j] = n p_j$
    
- **Variance:** $\operatorname{Var}(X_j) = n p_j (1 - p_j)$
    

This confirms the marginal expected value and variance you noted in the lecture.
